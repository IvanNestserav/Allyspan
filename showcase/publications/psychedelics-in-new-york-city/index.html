    Psychedelics in New York City

    By Ivan Nestserav

    14 November 2026 · Publication note and corrections: 15 November 2026

    COMMUNITY AWARENESS PUBLICATIONS | ALLYSPAN

    Publication note · 15 November 2026

    This is a community discussion paper and advocacy position, dated 14 November 2026. It is a

    narrative review, not an original clinical study, a peer-reviewed journal article, or medical advice. The

    full 28-page paper follows this note.

    How to read the Columbia critique. The paper raises questions about the scope of the evidence,

    trial eligibility, long-term outcomes and equitable access. It does not establish misconduct, a failure

    to follow an ongoing trial protocol, or that Columbia ignored a specific safety concern. Ongoing

    studies must not be described as completed findings.

    includes a Columbia-affiliated co-author. Columbia’s contribution should not be reduced to one trial

    RESEARCH ARTICLE  |  PUBLIC HEALTH, PSYCHIATRY AND DRUG POLICY Psychedelics in New York City

    Columbia University research, the national evidence, the dangers for people with mental-health conditions and for society, Allyspan's position, and the case for a community-centred - not commercial - model

    Prepared 14 November 2026  ·  New York City

    Evidence reviewed to November 2026. No psilocybin or MDMA therapy was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug

    Administration (FDA) at the time of writing.

    Abstract

    This article examines psychedelic research and policy in New York City, grounded in the work actually done at

    Columbia University, and tests the claims made for psychedelics against the published evidence. Columbia's

    contribution is real but narrow: David Hellerstein's Depression Evaluation Service at the New York State

    Psychiatric Institute was a site in the phase 2b trial of synthetic psilocybin (COMP360) for treatment-resistant

    depression, and Elias Dakwar's programme showed that a single ketamine infusion combined with therapy

    improved abstinence in alcohol use disorder. Columbia has no dedicated psychedelic centre and no MDMA trial

    of its own. Nationally, psilocybin trials show consistently positive but fragile effects that shrink as trials become

    larger and better controlled; MDMA-assisted therapy for post-traumatic stress disorder was refused by the FDA

    in August 2024. The risks are concentrated in exactly the people most likely to seek help - people with psychosis,

    bipolar disorder, suicidality and complex medication regimens - In New

    often excluded from major trials.

    York, the law has not changed: psilocybin remains Schedule I, no City Council decriminalisation resolution has

    passed, and four state bills stalled in committee in the 2025-26 session. Against this, tobacco kills more than

    480,000 Americans a year and excessive alcohol use about 178,000, with the burden falling hardest on people

    with mental illness, while cannabis carries a dose-dependent risk of psychosis. A commercial model priced on

    current industry guidance would exclude most New Yorkers.

    Allyspan, a developing community organisation,

    states its position in Section 9: its foundation is not capitalism, it sells and provides no psychedelic treatment,

    and it opposes a market built on unproven treatment. The article therefore argues for decriminalisation of

    possession, separated from commercial sale, with publicly funded screening, peer support and integration - and

    for honesty about what remains unproven.

    Keywords: psilocybin; MDMA; ketamine; Columbia University; New York City; schizophrenia; bipolar

    disorder; decriminalisation; health equity; harm reduction; psychiatric disability; Allyspan

    How to read this article. Numbers are given with their source, sample size and study type wherever the evidence allows. Findings taken from company press releases, trade press or advocacy sources are labelled as such and are

    never presented as peer-reviewed results. Claims that research for this article could not verify - including claims

    circulating online that a psychedelic bill passed the New York Senate, or that the City Council passed a decriminalisation resolution - are flagged in the text rather than repeated as fact.

    The honest verdict in one paragraph. The evidence is not strong enough to justify the claims commonly made for psychedelics - and it is not weak enough to prove they do not work. The direction of effect is consistently positive; the size of the effect falls whenever the trial gets bigger, the comparator gets stronger, or the follow-up gets longer. That pattern punctures the hype; it does not bury the field.

    Publication · 3

    1   Columbia University: a trial site, not a psychedelic centre

    Any account of psychedelics in New York City that begins at Columbia must begin with a correction. Columbia University has no dedicated centre for psychedelic research comparable to the centres at Johns Hopkins or Imperial

    College London, and it has no MDMA trial of its own. Its work runs through the Department of Psychiatry and the

    New York State Psychiatric Institute (NYSPI), principally through two programmes. Presenting Columbia as the home of a large psychedelic programme would inflate a modest, genuine contribution into something it is not.

    Psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression - Hellerstein

    David J. Hellerstein, MD, Professor of Clinical Psychiatry, directs the Depression Evaluation Service at NYSPI.

    The service was a participating site - and Hellerstein a co-author - in the largest psilocybin trial of its time:

    Goodwin and colleagues' phase 2b randomised trial of a single dose of synthetic psilocybin (COMP360,

    COMPASS Pathways) for treatment-resistant depression, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2022 (n=233, 22 sites).

    Participants received 25 mg, 10 mg or 1 mg (control) with psychological support, after coming off antidepressants.

    At week 3, depression scores (MADRS) had fallen by 12.0, 7.9 and 5.4 points respectively. The 25 mg dose beat the 1 mg control by 6.6 points (95% CI -10.2 to -2.9); 37% responded and 29% remitted. The 10 mg dose did not

    separate significantly from control. By week 12, only 20.3% of the 25 mg group remained responders, against

    10.1% of controls - about one in five. Adverse events occurred in 77% of participants, most commonly headache, nausea and dizziness. Suicidal ideation and intentional self-injury occurred in all groups, including the control

    group, and suicidal behaviours were reported at least a month after dosing in three non-responders in the 25 mg

    arm. The authors' own conclusion was restrained: larger and longer trials, including comparison with existing treatments, are required.

    The Columbia review

    Bender and Hellerstein's 2022 clinical review in Psychopharmacology assessed the second wave of psychedelic research: 14 trials since 1991 - 11 of psilocybin (257 participants), one of LSD (12) and two of ayahuasca (46). The

    review concluded that small numbers of treatments with psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy can offer significant

    and sustained alleviation across several conditions, and that no serious adverse events attributed to psychedelic therapy had been reported in those trials. It flagged its own limits in the same breath: small samples, the inherent

    difficulty of blinding, limited follow-up and highly screened populations. It remains the most quotable honest

    Columbia source on the field as a whole.

    Publication · 4

    1   Columbia University (continued)

    Ketamine for addiction - Dakwar

    Columbia's most original contribution comes from Elias Dakwar, MD, Associate Professor of Clinical Psychiatry in

    the Division on Substance Use Disorders. In a randomised, midazolam-controlled pilot trial published in the American Journal of Psychiatry in 2020, 40 adults with alcohol use disorder received a single sub-anaesthetic

    ketamine infusion (0.71 mg/kg) or midazolam during a five-week course of motivational enhancement therapy. At

    three weeks, 82% of the ketamine group remained abstinent, against 65% of controls; the ketamine group also took longer to relapse and had fewer heavy-drinking days. The effect lasted a few weeks. A secondary analysis found

    that mystical-type experience, not dissociation, mediated the effect on at-risk drinking. Dakwar's earlier laboratory

    work reported similar signals for cocaine dependence (2014, 2017). These are small pilot studies, not a treatment programme - and ketamine for depression at Columbia should not be confused with the separate, Janssen-owned

    evidence base for esketamine (Spravato).

    Newer and smaller work

    A small, open-label, uncontrolled 2025 study co-led from Columbia (Kelley and colleagues, JAMA Psychiatry)

    gave two psilocybin doses to people with Parkinson's disease and depression and found that five inflammation-

    linked signalling molecules in blood fell and stayed lower at 30 days. The lead author described it, accurately, as a potential jumping-off point for more research - not a treatment claim. Hellerstein's wider portfolio, as listed in

    programme biographies, includes psilocybin studies in body dysmorphic disorder and anorexia nervosa and work

    with 5-MeO-DMT. No outcome data for those studies were found; they should be described as ongoing or planned, never as results.

    What Columbia has not done. No MDMA trial, no LSD trial and no cancer-distress psilocybin trial at Columbia were

    found in this research. The landmark cancer-distress trials belong to Johns Hopkins (Griffiths et al., 2016) and to NYU

    Langone in this same city (Ross et al., 2016). Attributing the NYU work to Columbia would be an error, and this article

    does not make it.

    That narrowness matters for policy. When advocates invoke "Columbia research" to support a New York

    programme, the accurate claim is that Columbia psychiatrists helped test one company's psilocybin product in one

    indication, ran a well-designed ketamine pilot in addiction, and wrote a careful review urging caution. That is a foundation for further research. It is not a mandate for a market.

    Meta-analyses underline the fragility. Pooled effects look large when within-subject and waitlist comparisons are

    included, and shrink against active comparators. One 2026 meta-analysis of 13 trials (n=606) found a pooled effect

    that was not statistically significant once comparator type was modelled, with extreme heterogeneity (I-squared 96.9%). Certainty of evidence across reviews is rated low to moderate. No trial has compared psilocybin with an

    optimised standard treatment over 12 months in a representative population, and durability beyond three to six

    months is largely unproven.

    Cancer-related distress: the strongest signal, the smallest base

    In Griffiths and colleagues' 2016 Johns Hopkins trial (n=51, life-threatening cancer), a high psilocybin dose

    produced large falls in depression and anxiety; at six months, 78% remained depression responders and 83% anxiety responders. In Ross and colleagues' 2016 NYU trial (n=29), 60-80% maintained clinically significant

    reductions at 6.5 months, and 60-80% of survivors still responded at four to five years. These are the most durable

    results in the field - from 80 people in total, in crossover designs whose controlled comparison ends at five to seven weeks, in samples that were predominantly white and highly educated. Whether the active ingredient is the drug or

    the profound subjective experience it produces - mystical-experience scores predicted outcome - remains an open

    scientific question with direct consequences for whether the intervention can be standardised as a medicine at all.

    Publication · 6

    2   The national evidence (continued)

    Addiction: alcohol and tobacco

    Bogenschutz and colleagues (2022, JAMA Psychiatry, n=95) gave two psilocybin sessions with 12 weeks of

    psychotherapy for alcohol use disorder. Heavy-drinking days over 32 weeks were 9.7%, against 23.6% in the control group receiving the same therapy with diphenhydramine. About 94% of participants nevertheless guessed

    their assignment correctly, recruitment stopped early, and a 2025 Swiss trial (n=37) found no difference. The result

    has not been replicated at scale.

    The most quoted number in the field is also the least reliable. Johnson and colleagues' open-label tobacco pilot

    (2014) reported 80% biochemically verified abstinence at six months - from 15 highly motivated smokers, with no

    control group and no randomisation. For comparison, varenicline achieves roughly 33-35% at six months in controlled trials. A pilot of 15 people cannot carry the claim built on it, and no published randomised result for

    psilocybin in tobacco cessation was found. There is likewise no published phase 2 or 3 psilocybin trial for opioid

    use disorder.

    MDMA for PTSD: the cautionary tale

    The MDMA programme produced the largest effects in psychedelic medicine - effect sizes of 0.91 (MAPP1, n=90)

    and 0.7 (MAPP2, n=104), with about two-thirds of participants no longer meeting PTSD criteria. It also produced the field's clearest regulatory failure. On 4 June 2024 the FDA advisory committee voted 9-2 that the data did not

    show effectiveness and 10-1 that benefits did not outweigh risks. The reasons were methodological, not ideological:

    about 94% of MDMA recipients in MAPP2 knew or guessed their allocation, so the blind had effectively failed; expectancy and prior MDMA use biased the sample; durability beyond 18 weeks was unproven; safety data were

    incomplete, with therapists reportedly trained not to record certain effects; and misconduct at a trial site led to

    retraction of a pooled long-term analysis in August 2024. The FDA's complete response letter of August 2024 requested a new phase 3 trial and an independent audit. Trade press reported a resubmission in August 2026 without

    a new phase 3 trial; the FDA had not confirmed an approval or timeline as of November 2026. MDMA remains

    Schedule I and unapproved.

    Ketamine and LSD

    Esketamine (Spravato) is the one approved product in this space - FDA-approved in 2019 for treatment-resistant

    depression as an add-on, and in January 2025 as monotherapy - delivered only in certified clinics under a risk- management programme, with at least two hours of observation and a label stating it has not been shown to prevent

    suicide. One of its pivotal trials missed its primary endpoint. At-home compounded ketamine, sold by telehealth

    companies, rests on evidence a 2026 systematic review rated at critical risk of bias and very low certainty. For LSD, modern controlled evidence amounts to a single trial of 12 people in the Columbia review; the 1950s-60s literature

    does not meet modern standards and cannot support a present-day claim.

    3   New York City: the law has not changed

    Psilocybin and psilocin are Schedule I substances under New York Public Health Law section 3306 and under federal law. Possession of any amount is at minimum a class A misdemeanour (Penal Law section 220.03);

    possession of 25 mg or more of a pure hallucinogen is a class C felony, and sale of any amount is a class D felony.

    There is no statewide decriminalisation, no licensed therapy programme and no legal psilocybin product on sale in New York City. A 2023 state law would automatically align New York's scheduling with a future federal change -

    which matters only if and when federal agencies act.

    What is not known about use in the city

    There is no representative survey of psilocybin use in New York City, and this article does not invent one. The best

    proxies, labelled as proxies, are these. Nationally, the 2024 National Survey on Drug Use and Health - the first year

    to ask about psilocybin specifically - found past-year use by 2.78% of Americans aged 12 and over, roughly 8 million people, with users more likely to be white, male, younger and higher-income. New York State tables for

    2021-22 counted 484,000 residents who had used any hallucinogen in the past year. Sentinel samples in the city run

    far higher and describe specific scenes, not the city: a 2025 street-recruited sample (n=239, not representative) reported 15.5% past-year psilocybin use, and nightclub and festival surveys by Palamar and colleagues found

    shroom use rising between 2017 and 2022. United States seizures of psilocybin mushrooms rose from 402 in 2017

    to 1,396 in 2022 - a supply signal, not a prevalence measure.

    The Council resolution that never passed

    Decriminalize Nature NYC drafted an Oakland-style resolution that would have made entheogenic plants and fungi the lowest law-enforcement priority in the city. As of reporting in May 2021 it had been shared with Council

    members with no timeline for a vote. No Council vote or passage was found through November 2026, and multiple

    2026 legal trackers agree that New York City has passed no formal decriminalisation resolution and that the city's

    district attorneys have announced no formal deprioritisation policy for psilocybin. Claims on low-quality websites that New York City decriminalised mushrooms in 2018 or 2021 conflate the city with Ann Arbor and other

    jurisdictions. They are not supported by any Council record found in this research, and they should not be repeated.

    Enforcement: the marijuana record is the warning

    No psilocybin-specific arrest breakdown for New York City was found. The closest analogue - marijuana, whose

    enforcement history any psychedelic reform inherits - is stark. NYPD figures show marijuana possession arrests falling from 53,529 in 2010 to 19,139 in 2017, yet in 2017 Black and Hispanic New Yorkers were still 86.9% of

    arrests for public burning. The City Comptroller's 2018 analysis found East Harlem's arrest rate was 13 times the

    Upper East Side's, and that the ten lowest-income neighbourhoods accounted for 34.3% of all marijuana arrests, against 11.0% for the ten highest-income. Between 1997 and 2006 the city arrested more than 353,000 people for

    small-amount marijuana possession, about 85% of them Black or Latino. After cannabis legalisation in 2021,

    enforcement moved to unlicensed shops - and sweeps of those shops also caught storefronts selling psilocybin

    edibles. The lesson generalises: decriminalising possession without addressing supply does not end enforcement. It moves enforcement to sellers, who are again disproportionately Black and Latino.

    What exists legally in the city today is ketamine clinics, esketamine in certified clinics, psychedelic integration

    therapy - talking therapy about a past experience, which is lawful - and clinical trials at Columbia/NYSPI, NYU Langone, Mount Sinai and other academic sites. Alongside them sits an underground of facilitators, delivery

    services and unlicensed storefronts, with untested product, unstandardised dosing and no recourse when boundaries

    are violated.

    4   Tobacco, alcohol and cannabis: the harms already here

    The strongest public-health argument in this field is comparative. The substances that are legal - or, for cannabis in New York, newly legal - already do damage on a scale psychedelics do not approach, and that damage falls hardest

    on people with mental-health conditions, the same people psychedelic therapy proposes to treat. The comparison

    exposes an incoherent legal hierarchy. It does not, by itself, make psychedelics safe.

    Tobacco: the largest preventable killer, concentrated in mental illness

    Tobacco kills more than 480,000 people a year in the United States - about one death in five - and cost more than $600 billion in 2018. In 2022, 11.6% of adults (28.8 million people) smoked. Prevalence tracks disadvantage at

    every step: 18.3% among low-income adults against 6.7% among high-income adults; 21.5% on Medicaid against

    8.6% with private insurance; 30.7% among holders of a GED against 3.2% among those with a graduate degree

    (CDC).

    The mental-health gradient is the point most relevant here. Adults with severe psychological distress smoke at

    28.1%, against 10.9% without. Adults ever told they had depression smoke at 19.4%, against 9.9%. Nearly one-

    third - 31% - of all cigarettes smoked by American adults are smoked by people with mental illness. Smoking is roughly two to three times as prevalent among people with mental illness overall, and about five times as prevalent

    among people with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, PTSD and substance use disorders; roughly two-thirds of

    people with schizophrenia and about half of people with bipolar disorder smoke. People with serious mental illness die on average about 25 years prematurely, primarily from chronic diseases including tobacco-related disease. Any

    honest discussion of drugs and mental health in New York begins here, not with mushrooms.

    Alcohol: 178,000 deaths a year, and a suicide amplifier

    Excessive alcohol use killed about 178,000 people a year in the United States in 2020-21 - more than 488 deaths a

    day, up 29% from 2016-17 - including one in five deaths among adults aged 20 to 49. The 2010 cost was $249

    billion, three-quarters of it from binge drinking. Alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen linked causally to at least seven cancers, accounting for more than 20,000 American cancer deaths a year.

    Publication · 10

    4   Tobacco, alcohol and cannabis (continued)

    Comorbidity is the norm, not the exception. In the 2024 national survey, 61.5 million adults had a mental illness in the past year; 34.5% of them (21.2 million) also had a substance use disorder. Among adults with serious mental

    illness, the figure was 47.3%. Alcohol and depression reinforce each other, and the combination is dangerous: in a

    treatment-sample case-control study, substance-induced depression was associated with 3.73 times the odds of a proximal suicide attempt, and independent depression with 10.38 times the odds. Heavy drinkers carry roughly five

    times the suicide risk of social drinkers, about 40% of people seeking treatment for alcohol dependence report a

    lifetime suicide attempt, and co-occurring alcohol use disorder with major depression is described in the literature as the most common comorbidity among people who die by suicide. This is the population - depressed, drinking,

    suicidal - that psychedelic trials systematically exclude, and that any real-world New York service would meet first.

    Cannabis: lower acute lethality, real psychiatric risk, rising potency

    Cannabis has no overdose mortality comparable to alcohol or opioids. Its psychiatric risk is nonetheless the best-

    quantified of any drug in this article. Between 22% and 30% of people who use cannabis develop cannabis use

    disorder - about three in ten, in the CDC's phrasing. For psychosis, the evidence shows a consistent dose-response gradient. Ever-use raises the risk of a psychotic outcome by about 40% (Moore et al., Lancet, 2007). The heaviest

    users carry an odds ratio of 3.90 against non-users in a meta-analysis of 66,816 people (Marconi et al., 2016). In the

    EU-GEI study across 11 sites (901 first-episode psychosis cases, 1,237 controls), daily use carried an odds ratio of 3.2, and daily use of high-potency cannabis (10% THC or more) an odds ratio of 4.8; the modelled share of cases

    attributable to high-potency use reached 30.3% in London and 50.3% in Amsterdam, on the assumption of causality

    (Di Forti et al., Lancet Psychiatry, 2019). In Denmark, the share of schizophrenia attributable to cannabis use disorder rose from about 2% in the mid-1990s to 6-8% in the 2010s as potency and use rose.

    Two cautions keep this honest. First, absolute risk stays small: even on causal assumptions, lifetime schizophrenia

    risk in regular users is about 2% - roughly 98% of regular users will not develop schizophrenia - though risk is

    much higher with genetic vulnerability and potent products. Second, causality runs in both directions: genetic liability to schizophrenia also predicts cannabis use. Neither caution erases the gradient. The same plant that is now

    a legal, taxed, venture-backed industry in New York was, within living memory, the pretext for the arrest wave

    described in Section 3. That history is the central equity warning for psychedelics.

    5   Commercialisation: who would be priced out

    The commercial question is not abstract. COMPASS Pathways, founded in 2016 and listed on NASDAQ, runs the largest psilocybin programme in history and was completing a rolling FDA submission in late 2026, with a targeted

    launch in the first half of 2027 if approved and rescheduled. Its core patents cover a crystalline form of psilocybin

    (Polymorph A, U.S. Patent 10,519,175) and oral formulations; a pending application at one stage claimed features of the therapy room itself - muted colours, soft furniture, music, a therapist holding the patient's hand. Challenges

    argued that the crystalline product was not novel in light of Albert Hofmann's 1958 work for Sandoz; the U.S.

    Patent Trial and Appeal Board declined to institute the late-2021 reviews, and the European Patent Office rejected a related patent. Defenders reply that patents are what fund FDA trials. Critics reply that a patent estate over a

    substance with centuries of Indigenous ceremonial use - Mazatec and other traditions, documented for Western

    science in the 1950s - monopolises a product of nature and of other people's knowledge. The nonprofit Usona Institute is the standing counter-model: it publishes its synthesis routes openly, pledges not to enforce certain

    patents, and, according to trade press in November 2026, submitted its own application for psilocybin in major

    depression. That submission is trade-press reporting, not an FDA record, and is stated here as such.

    The price decides who is treated

    At an investor summit in September 2026, COMPASS management guided that COMP360 would be priced at

    about $50,000-$65,000 per patient per year, benchmarked to Spravato - in the chief executive's words, priced on that annual figure divided by three doses, or roughly $17,000-$22,000 per dose, before the cost of about six hours

    of supervised monitoring per dose. No list price, label or coverage decision existed as of November 2026; this is

    management guidance, not a price. Set it against the economics: a 2025 cost-effectiveness model found psilocybin- assisted therapy cost-effective only at a course price of $5,000 or less. The gap between a $5,000 cost-effective

    course and a $50,000 commercial year is the central access fact in this field.

    Oregon, the only operating licensed model, shows what an unregulated price does. Licensed sessions cost

    $1,000-$3,500, with a median of $1,900 in a 2025 mystery-shopper study of 22 centres. Facilitator training costs $7,000-$12,000, the facilitator licence $2,000 a year and a service-centre licence $10,000 a year; the Oregon Health

    Authority has acknowledged it has no authority to set the price of services, and no insurance covers them.

    Meanwhile the people who already use psilocybin are disproportionately white, male, younger and more affluent. A commercial model selects further on wealth. A decriminalisation-only model selects on risk tolerance and social

    capital. Neither reaches, on its own, the New Yorkers with the highest burden of depression, PTSD and addiction -

    disproportionately Black, Latino and low-income, and disproportionately the targets of past enforcement.

    6   The dangers, condition by condition

    One pattern runs through every condition in this section. In screened, supervised trials, serious psychiatric harms are uncommon - because the trials achieve that safety by excluding almost everyone at highest risk: personal or

    family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, active suicidality, active substance use disorder, unstable medical

    disease and, for classic psychedelics, lithium use. Trial safety figures therefore describe screened volunteers, not the population that would present to a New York service. Outside trials, the evidence is thinner - case reports, self-

    selected surveys and registry studies - and this section grades it as it goes. No psychedelic is FDA-approved for any

    condition discussed here except esketamine for treatment-resistant depression; MDMA for PTSD was refused in August 2024.

    6.1   Schizophrenia and psychotic disorders - the highest-concern contraindication

    The standard safety guidelines (Johnson, Richards and Griffiths, 2008) recommend excluding anyone with a current or past psychotic disorder, bipolar I or II, or a first- or second-degree relative with those disorders, with

    antipsychotic medication available as rescue. The rationale is direct: prolonged psychoses triggered by

    hallucinogens are reported, and screening is, in the guidelines' words, the key factor in minimising that risk.

    Inside screened trials the incidence is low. A 2025 meta-analysis (Wolf et al., Molecular Psychiatry) put

    psychedelic-induced psychosis at 0.2% in uncontrolled trials and 0.6% in randomised trials, and 0.002% in

    population studies. Those figures must not be quoted as the risk for a person with schizophrenia: they are pooled across mostly screened populations. In the uncontrolled trials that did include people with schizophrenia, 3.8%

    developed long-lasting psychotic symptoms. A 2025 systematic review of 52 papers found that 58% of case reports

    of psychedelic-related psychosis involved a personal or family psychiatric history, and that first-episode psychosis in otherwise healthy people, while documented, is rare.

    The sharpest quantitative warning comes from what happens after a substance-induced psychosis. Murrie and

    colleagues' meta-analysis (2020, 50 studies, 40,783 people) found that 25% of substance-induced psychoses were

    later diagnosed as schizophrenia - 26% for hallucinogens and 34% for cannabis, the highest of any substance. Most transitions occurred within the first few years. Conversion does not prove the drug caused schizophrenia; it may

    unmask latent vulnerability or reflect a first episode initially mislabelled. It is still the best warning available, and

    hallucinogens sit just below cannabis in it. Mechanistically, classic psychedelics act on serotonin 5-HT2A receptors in ways that increase glutamate release and disrupt sensory gating, and LSD has an additional later dopaminergic

    phase - dopamine hyperactivity being the classic schizophrenia mechanism. That is a plausible mechanism, not a

    proven causal chain. Ketamine, separately, can worsen symptoms in schizophrenia and produce short-term psychosis at high doses.

    6   The dangers (continued)

    6.2   Bipolar disorder - mania is the defining risk

    Bipolar I is essentially untested and universally excluded, on the precaution that serotonin agonism, sleep

    disruption and the intensity of the experience can flip a stable person into mania. The case literature supports the precaution: a 2023 review collected 18 cases of mania after psychedelics, five involving psilocybin, including

    psychotic mania about 36 hours after a single mushroom ingestion in a young woman with a family history of

    bipolar disorder, and mania with psychotic features two days after ayahuasca in people with prior hypomania or family history.

    The largest bipolar-specific dataset is a naturalistic survey (Morton et al., 2023; n=541 people with self-reported

    bipolar disorder who had taken psilocybin to a full trip). In the 14 days afterwards, 32.2% reported new or worsening symptoms - prominently manic symptoms, sleep difficulty and anxiety - and 3.3% (18 people) used

    emergency services. There was no difference between bipolar I and II. Most respondents still rated psilocybin more

    helpful than harmful overall. The survey was self-selected and diagnoses were self-reported, so 32.2% is a signal of

    frequency among users who chose to respond, not a population incidence.

    The counterpoint is a single small trial. Aaronson and colleagues (2024, JAMA Psychiatry) gave one 25 mg

    psilocybin dose with psychotherapy to 15 people with treatment-resistant bipolar II depression. Depression

    improved markedly, mania rating scores did not change significantly, and no psychosis occurred over 12 weeks. The authors state explicitly that the findings cannot be extrapolated to bipolar I, or to bipolar II in a mixed or

    hypomanic phase. Fifteen carefully selected people do not overturn the exclusion - and lithium, the standard bipolar

    stabiliser, is itself a contraindication, for reasons given in Section 6.9.

    6.3   Depression with suicidality - benefit unproven where risk is highest

    Depression trials exclude people judged at clinically significant suicide risk, yet suicidality still appears in the data.

    In Goodwin 2022, suicidal ideation, suicidal behaviour or self-injury occurred in all dose groups, including the 1 mg control: in the weeks after dosing, suicidal ideation was reported by 6% at 25 mg, 5% at 10 mg and 3% at 1 mg,

    with intentional self-injury and, in the 25 mg arm, three cases of suicidal behaviour. Causality is not established -

    the population was treatment-resistant, with high baseline suicidality, and events occurred on the control dose too. But the trial equally does not show that psilocybin protects against suicidality, and its week-3 benefit was not

    sustained at week 12. Worsening after a non-response is a real and under-studied harm: among 1,993 reports of

    worst-ever psilocybin experiences (Carbonaro et al., 2016), three attempted suicides were recorded, and suicidal ideation appears among the extended difficulties in the survey literature. The practical conclusion is uncomfortable

    and necessary: the suicidal depressed population is the one least represented in the safety data and most likely to

    seek these treatments first.

    6   The dangers (continued)

    6.4   Anxiety and panic - the commonest harm, acute and persistent

    Acute anxiety is not a side effect separable from these drugs; it is part of their action. Psilocybin amplifies the

    emotional state brought into the session, and loss of control, ego dissolution and feared material surfacing are intrinsic to the experience. In pooled data from 110 healthy volunteers, anxiety or fear occurred in about one-third

    during high-dose sessions despite preparation. In Carbonaro's worst-trip survey, 39% rated the experience among

    the five most challenging of their lives and 11% put themselves or others at risk of physical harm; risk rose with dose, duration, difficulty and the absence of physical comfort and social support. Afterwards, 24% reported at least

    one symptom - anxiety, paranoia, depression or fear - lasting a week or more, and 10% reported symptoms lasting

    over a year. Persistent anxiety is the commonest prolonged difficulty in every survey that measures it, and a pre- existing anxiety disorder predicted adverse mental-health effects after ayahuasca in the global ayahuasca survey.

    The literature's distinction between a "challenging" experience (framed as therapeutic) and a harmful one

    (prolonged distress) is drawn retrospectively, after the outcome is known - which is precisely why it cannot be used in advance to reassure an anxious patient.

    6.5   PTSD - flooding, retraumatisation, and a refused approval

    MDMA has the strongest efficacy signal of any psychedelic indication, and its safety record in trials was mostly mild-to-moderate: psychiatric adverse events in 83.0% of the MDMA arm against 72.5% on placebo in MAPP2,

    with suicidal ideation, insomnia and anxiety most frequent, and single severe cases of dissociation, flashback and

    grief reaction. The trials nevertheless excluded dissociative identity disorder, primary psychotic disorder, bipolar I and current substance use disorders - that is, much of complex PTSD as it presents in practice. Blood pressure

    reached 140/90 or above in 68% of MDMA recipients against 22% on placebo in the FDA's pooled analysis, and

    systolic pressure reached 180 or above in 6% against none.

    Retraumatisation is a recognised clinical concern with a clear mechanism - these drugs lower defensive avoidance

    and surface traumatic material - supported by case-level and survey evidence linking difficult experiences,

    especially in people with early trauma, to later emotional dysregulation, derealisation and PTSD symptoms

    following psychedelic use. No controlled incidence figure exists, and this article does not invent one. The FDA refusal (Section 2) turned substantially on safety-data quality: effects such as euphoria were reportedly not captured

    as adverse events, liver tests were not collected, and cardiac assessment was incomplete. A therapy whose harms

    were not fully counted cannot be declared safe on its own trial data.

    6   The dangers (continued)

    6.6   OCD - the one condition leaning toward benefit, on very small numbers

    Obsessive-compulsive disorder is the exception in this section, and honesty requires saying so. Moreno and

    colleagues (2006) gave up to four psilocybin doses to nine people with OCD and no other current major psychiatric disorder; all showed marked falls in symptom scores (23-100%) during at least one session, generally lasting

    beyond 24 hours, with one case of transient hypertension and no other significant adverse effects. A 2026

    randomised trial from the same group (n=15, repeated weekly doses against an active comparator) reported 73.3% response and 40% remission by week 8, with no serious adverse events and no psychotic symptoms. No published

    case series of psilocybin worsening OCD was found. The risks are theoretical - amplification of intrusive

    obsessions during the session - plus a practical one: entering a trial can require tapering the SSRIs that blunt psilocybin, risking severe OCD rebound. Two trials totalling 24 people are a favourable early signal, not a

    treatment. OCD should be presented as investigational, not as a contraindication - and not as a proven therapy.

    6.7   Eating disorders - the most medically fragile population

    The only published anorexia trial is a feasibility study: Peck and colleagues (2023, Nature Medicine) gave a single

    25 mg dose to ten women with anorexia nervosa or partial remission (mean BMI 19.7). Adverse events were mild

    and transient, with two cases of asymptomatic low blood sugar; four of ten improved clinically at three months. The study was designed to test safety and feasibility, not efficacy, and had no control group. The specific dangers are

    medical: anorexia brings cardiac muscle wasting, electrolyte disturbance, QT prolongation and low blood sugar,

    against a drug that transiently raises heart rate and blood pressure - which is why trials require medical stability and cardiac clearance. Purging in bulimia adds electrolyte risk. A 2026 single-arm pilot (21 women, three sessions;

    reported in secondary summaries) recorded one participant making two suicide attempts seven and nine months

    after treatment - causality not established, in the psychiatric condition with the highest mortality of any, at roughly 5-10% per decade. No published psilocybin trial in bulimia or binge-eating disorder was found.

    6.8   Personality disorders, especially borderline personality disorder

    Here the evidence is almost entirely absence. Major trials - Goodwin 2022, Carhart-Harris 2021, the MDMA programme - excluded primary personality-disorder diagnoses, and COMPASS phase 3 protocols exclude

    borderline, antisocial and several other personality disorders. The exclusion is precautionary, based on fit:

    borderline personality disorder involves intense, rapidly shifting affect, identity disturbance, dissociation, impulsivity and recurrent suicidality, and a six-to-eight-hour, highly intimate, boundary-dissolving session with two

    therapists, followed by weeks of integration, is a setting in which attachment intensity, idealisation and devaluation,

    and boundary violations are specifically feared. The misconduct episode in the MDMA programme - therapists restraining a distressed participant - is cited in exactly this context. No controlled study quantifies any of it. The

    first direct test, a phase 1 trial of two psilocybin doses in 18 adults with borderline personality disorder already

    established in dialectical behaviour therapy, was registered in 2026 and had not begun recruiting; active suicidal intent remains an exclusion even there. Established treatments - DBT, mentalisation-based and transference-focused

    therapy, schema therapy - are what the evidence supports today.

    6   The dangers (continued)

    6.9   Dissociation, substance use, autism - and the interaction that can seize

    Dissociative disorders. Ketamine causes dissociation by design; Krystal and colleagues showed in 1994 that sub-

    anaesthetic ketamine reliably induces depersonalisation and derealisation. Classic psychedelics can leave the same states behind: depersonalisation and derealisation are among the commonest extended difficulties in the survey

    literature, and in qualitative interviews 46.7% described disconnection or derealisation, including reality "feeling

    thin". There is no established role for ketamine in depersonalisation/derealisation disorder, and inducing a dissociative state in someone whose core suffering is a chronic unwanted dissociative state is counter-therapeutic

    on its face. Trials exclude dissociative identity disorder. No benefit data exist for this group.

    Active substance use disorders. Active use is excluded from almost all trials - not because psychedelics are proven harmful in addiction, but because intoxication and withdrawal endanger a long session (alcohol and

    benzodiazepine withdrawal can seize; stimulant intoxication raises heart rate and blood pressure), interactions

    multiply, consent and engagement are impaired, and the experience itself can trigger relapse. The distinction that

    matters is between active, unstable use - a contraindication - and sustained remission, which is the population in the promising alcohol and tobacco trials. Ketamine carries the clearest abuse liability of the drugs discussed here, with

    psychological dependence, tolerance and compulsive use; MDMA's dopaminergic action raises a lesser version of

    the same concern.

    Autism. Almost nothing is known. The single randomised trial is MDMA for social anxiety in 12 autistic adults

    (Danforth et al., 2018): social anxiety scores fell more on MDMA than placebo and the effect persisted at six

    months, with no serious adverse events but anxiety during sessions in 75% on MDMA against 25% on placebo - in a sample of 12, with near-total unblinding. No published psilocybin trial in autism exists. A self-selected 2024

    survey of 233 autistic adults found most reporting benefit, but about 20% reported harms including increased

    anxiety. The central hypothesised risk is sensory overwhelm: psychedelics loosen the brain's sensory filtering, and

    for a sensory system already operating near capacity that can tip into genuine distress. No study has tested dose or setting adaptations for autistic people. Both the benefit and the risk here are theoretical plus anecdotal, and should

    be labelled as such.

    Lithium - the hard stop. In a retrospective analysis of online experience reports (Nayak et al., 2021), 29 of 62 reports (46.8%) combining lithium with a classic psychedelic involved a seizure - 27 of 55 for LSD and 2 of 6 for

    psilocybin - and 39% required medical attention. Lamotrigine, by contrast, showed no seizures in 34 reports. The

    data are self-reported and biased toward dramatic outcomes, but the signal is large and consistent enough that lithium is a near-universal trial exclusion and Oregon's licensed programme mandates it. A low dose of lithium is

    not a workaround on current evidence. For a person with bipolar disorder, this closes the door twice: the condition

    is excluded, and so is its standard treatment.

    6.10   Other interactions, HPPD, and prolonged difficulties

    Antidepressants. For psilocybin, the documented SSRI and SNRI interaction is blunting, not serotonin syndrome:

    in a survey of 611 mushroom-use reports, effects were weaker than expected in about 47% of SSRI users and 55% of SNRI users. Short-term escitalopram before psilocybin preserved positive effects and reduced unpleasant ones in

    a small controlled study, and scoping reviews find combinations generally tolerated in supervised settings. Nobody

    should stop an antidepressant abruptly to feel a psychedelic more strongly: discontinuation symptoms and relapse are the predictable result, and raising the psychedelic dose to overcome blunting is unsafe because cardiovascular

    effects persist. For MDMA the picture is more dangerous: SSRIs blunt its subjective effects by roughly 30-80%

    while blood levels, heart rate, blood pressure and temperature effects persist - inviting redosing to chase an effect that cannot arrive. MAOIs are the dangerous combination: ayahuasca is itself an MAOI combined with DMT,

    and combining it with SSRIs, tramadol, dextromethorphan or stimulants risks serotonin syndrome; MDMA with an

    MAOI, including moclobemide, has killed. Antipsychotics block psilocybin and LSD at the receptor, which is why they serve as rescue medication - and why needing one marks the population trials exclude. Cardiovascular risk is

    real across all five drugs, most pronounced for MDMA, and uncontrolled hypertension, coronary disease,

    arrhythmia and recent stroke or heart attack are standard exclusions. Active epilepsy is likewise excluded; beyond

    lithium, antidepressants, mood stabilisers, bupropion and tramadol all appear in seizure reports or lower the seizure threshold.

    HPPD. Hallucinogen persisting perception disorder - lasting visual snow, trails, afterimages and intensified colours,

    causing distress - comes in a brief, often undistressing flashback form and a continuous, impairing form. Prevalence figures are soft: the DSM-5 cites 4.2% of hallucinogen users experiencing HPPD-like symptoms; controlled studies

    suggest 1-3% meet full criteria, and recent reviews converge on under 1-4% for the clinically significant form. No

    population-based study exists, no case was observed in modern clinical trials beyond a single recent report, and cases after one exposure in people without prior illness are documented. LSD is most often implicated; flares are

    triggered by stress, renewed use including cannabis and alcohol, and sleep deprivation. There is no approved

    treatment; abstinence is the primary intervention.

    Prolonged difficulties. The two denominators in this literature must not be confused. In a general sample of 3,168 American adults with lifetime psychedelic use (Simonsson et al., 2025), 87.9% reported no difficulties, 6.4%

    reported difficulties lasting more than a day and 1.3% more than a year; among those with difficulties, the

    commonest were general anxiety (33.9%), negative changes in self-concept (25.9%) and social disconnection (23.0%), and four or more adverse childhood experiences roughly doubled to tripled the odds of prolonged

    difficulty. In a self-selected sample of 608 people who specifically reported extended difficulties (Evans et al.,

    2023), about a third were still affected after a year and a sixth after three years. The first set of numbers describes how often it happens; the second describes how bad it can get. Predictors recur across studies: prior anxiety,

    psychosis or personality-disorder diagnosis, childhood adversity, high or unknown dose, an unsupportive setting,

    and no preparation, integration or sober sitter. Most respondents, even after difficulty, still rate the experience as beneficial in retrospect - a fact to report alongside the harms, not as a rebuttal of them.

    7   The efficacy case does not meet the bar for endorsement

    Sections 2 and 6 leave a reader with two separate questions. Do these drugs work well enough to endorse? And are they safe enough, for the people most likely to receive them, to build a service on? Tested against the standard

    applied to any other psychiatric treatment, the answer to both questions is currently no. That is not a rhetorical

    verdict. It is the verdict regulators, an independent evidence review and the psychiatric profession have already reached on the record.

    The tests a new treatment must pass - and where this one fails them

    Test What the record shows

    Blinding holds, so the result is not expectation

    It does not hold. About 94% of participants in the psilocybin alcohol trial guessed their assignment correctly; in the MDMA trials about 90% of the drug group and 75% of the placebo group guessed correctly (FDA briefing, 2024). A result measured in people who know what they took cannot separate drug effect from hope.

    The effect survives larger, better trials

    It shrinks. Psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression: -6.6 MADRS points in phase 2b (n=233), then -3.6 and -3.8 points in phase 3 - and the phase 3 figures are company press releases, not peer-reviewed papers, with response redefined at a 25% threshold instead of the conventional 50%.

    The benefit lasts Unproven. Only 20.3% of the 25 mg group in the phase 2b trial were still responders at week 12. The FDA told the MDMA sponsor it had not shown an effect durable beyond its 18-week assessments.

    It beats, or matches, treatment already available

    Not shown. Against escitalopram (n=59) the primary outcome difference was not significant. No trial compares psilocybin with an optimised standard treatment over 12 months in a representative population.

    Independent synthesis confirms the effect

    It does not. A 2026 meta-analysis of 13 trials (n=606) found a pooled effect that was not statistically significant once comparator type was modelled, with extreme heterogeneity (I-squared 96.9%). Certainty across reviews is rated low to moderate.

    What the authorities said, in their own decisions

    The regulator. On 4 June 2024 the FDA's Psychopharmacologic Drugs Advisory Committee voted 9-2 that the

    MDMA data did not show effectiveness, and 10-1 that the benefits did not outweigh the risks. In August 2024 the FDA refused approval and required an additional phase 3 trial. Its complete response letter raised functional

    unblinding, expectancy, durability and the reliability of the safety data, including the training of therapists not to

    record effects such as euphoria as adverse events.

    The independent reviewer. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review rated the MDMA evidence

    "insufficient" in June 2024 and declined to issue a price benchmark - the rating it gives when the evidence cannot

    support a judgement that a treatment works.

    The profession. The American Psychiatric Association's position statement (July 2022) holds that "there is currently inadequate scientific evidence for endorsing the use of psychedelics to treat any psychiatric disorder

    except within the context of approved investigational studies", and that clinical treatment should be decided by

    scientific evidence and regulatory standards, "not by ballot initiatives or popular opinion". No psilocybin or MDMA product had FDA approval at the time of writing.

    The integrity of the record. In August 2024 Psychopharmacology retracted a pooled analysis of six phase 2

    MDMA trials, citing protocol violations amounting to unethical conduct at one study site that the authors knew of and did not disclose. A literature that loses a pooled analysis to undisclosed misconduct has to rebuild trust before it

    can ask for a market.

    What this section does - and does not - prove. It crushes the claim that psychedelics are a proven breakthrough ready for

    rollout, endorsement or sale. That claim fails on blinding, on durability, on comparison with existing care, on independent

    synthesis and at the regulator. It does not prove the drugs have no effect: Raison (2023) found a -12.3 point difference, and

    the cancer-distress results are large. The honest position is the one the evidence forces - unproven at the standard required,

    and therefore not a basis for treating, charging or marketing to vulnerable people.

    8   The dangers at scale: society and psychiatric disability

    Section 6 describes what can happen to one person. This section describes what happens when a powerful, unproven intervention meets a whole city - and why the costs fall first and hardest on people with mental-health

    conditions and psychiatric disabilities: the group this article will call, respectfully and precisely, people with

    psychiatric disabilities, meaning people whose mental-health condition substantially limits daily life, work, housing or care.

    Excluded from the trials, exposed in the market

    This is the central structural danger. Trials stay safe by excluding people with psychosis or bipolar history, active

    suicidality, active substance use, unstable medical illness and lithium use. A commercial service then faces a choice

    it cannot escape: exclude the same people, and deny care to those in greatest need while selling to the least ill; or

    include them, and expose them to risks that have never been measured in them. Either way, people with psychiatric disabilities lose - shut out of the advertised benefit, or admitted to the unmeasured risk. Marketing does not respect

    that boundary: hype reaches everyone, including people a clinic would screen out, and it reaches them as a reason

    to seek the drug underground, without screening at all.

    Consent and power in an altered state

    A person under a psychedelic cannot withdraw consent the way a sober patient can: they may be unable to speak clearly, judge who is touching them, or leave. The ethics literature on psychedelic-assisted therapy documents

    sexual violations and abuses of power in exactly this setting (Harrison et al., American Journal of Bioethics, 2025).

    The retracted MDMA analysis in Section 7 arose from undisclosed unethical conduct at a trial site. For people whose disability already involves trauma, dissociation, impaired communication or dependence on carers, the

    power gap between a facilitator and a person in an altered state is wider, and the route to complain afterwards is

    harder. Any model that cannot guarantee independent oversight, a second professional present, recorded

    accountability and a real complaints route has no business treating this population.

    A two-tier city, measured where it has already happened

    Oregon is the test case, and its own data settle the equity question. State reporting for the first quarter of 2025 showed that, among clients who disclosed income, the majority earned more than $95,000 a year, against a state

    median household income of about $88,000; sessions commonly cost $1,000-$3,500 and are not covered by

    insurance. Disability cuts access a second way: facilitators sued Oregon in 2024 alleging that requiring all use to happen inside licensed centres violates the Americans with Disabilities Act by excluding homebound and terminally

    ill people. A service that is too expensive for low-income New Yorkers and physically inaccessible to homebound

    New Yorkers is not a mental-health advance for disabled people. It is a benefit for the well, described in the language of the unwell.

    8   The dangers at scale (continued)

    Enforcement, records and collateral harm in New York

    New Yorkers with psychiatric disabilities already meet the state at its harshest points. In the city's jails, about 60%

    of people held have a documented mental-health need and 22% a serious mental illness (Correctional Health Services data, reported November 2025); 42% of those with a serious mental illness were homeless or likely to be

    homeless on release. In a population this exposed, every new drug policy carries collateral risks that do not appear

    in a trial report: a possession stop becoming a pretext search, a screening record affecting housing, benefits, employment or child-welfare decisions, and a crisis during an unsupervised experience becoming an arrest or an

    involuntary admission rather than care. Section 3 showed, from the marijuana record, that this city has run exactly

    that pattern before.

    Scarce care diverted, and a society taught the wrong lesson

    Every positive trial bundled the drug with more than ten hours of skilled human time - preparation, supervised

    dosing and integration. Therapists, psychiatrists and crisis workers are the scarcest resource in New York mental- health care. Directing them to high-fee sessions for screened, paying clients withdraws them from the people with

    schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and severe depression whom the trials excluded and the clinics already fail. The

    money follows the same path: public or charitable funds spent on an unproven, patent-priced intervention are funds not spent on what is known to protect this population - housing, peer support, resource navigation, tobacco and

    alcohol treatment, and continuity of ordinary psychiatric care for people who already die 10-25 years earlier than

    the general population.

    There is also a cultural cost. Hype lowers perceived risk. In the sentinel New York nightclub and festival

    population, about one in ten past-year mushroom users reported a harmful or very unpleasant effect in which they

    feared for their immediate safety. A society that announces a breakthrough before it has one will get more unsupervised use, more mixing with alcohol, cannabis and prescribed medicines, more emergency presentations -

    and, when the promised cure does not arrive, deeper distrust of mental-health care among the people who need it

    most.

    The societal verdict. At scale, the dangers are not random. They are patterned: exclusion in the evidence, exposure in the market, power without protection in the session, price and physical access that shut disabled people out, enforcement and records that punish them afterwards, and scarce care diverted away from them throughout. That pattern - not any single adverse event - is the case against commercial rollout in New York City now.

    9   Allyspan's position: not built on capitalism

    This section is a position statement. It is Allyspan's opinion, stated plainly and attributed to Allyspan alone; it is not a scientific finding, and the evidence for and against each part of it is the evidence set out in Sections 1-8.

    Allyspan's slogan is "Support That Stays Beside You."

    Allyspan is developing as a New York community organisation with charitable and

    What Allyspan is. educational purposes including poverty relief, opposition to discrimination, resource navigation, peer and

    community support and nonformal workshops - support that stays beside people while they find their next step. Its

    founding documents deliberately provide for no school, no healthcare or mental-health programme, no psychotherapy, no diagnosis and no treatment. Allyspan is not a clinic, does not sell, provide or facilitate

    psychedelic treatment, and has no financial interest in any drug, patent or therapy business. Its opposition below is

    therefore not a competitor's pitch. It is the position of a community organisation with nothing to sell.

    Its foundation is not capitalism. Allyspan states this without qualification: it is not founded on capitalism, and it rejects a model in which relief from suffering is a product, access is set by price, and value returns to investors and

    patent holders. A treatment projected at about $50,000-$65,000 per patient per year, or sold at $1,000-$3,500 a

    session to clients who mostly earn more than the median household, is that model working exactly as designed. Allyspan opposes it - not reform of its prices, but the foundation itself.

    Allyspan's position, point by point

    No endorsement before proof. Allyspan does not endorse psychedelics for any mental-health condition. It stands with the American Psychiatric Association: the scientific evidence is currently inadequate outside approved investigational studies.

    No market on disability. People with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, suicidality and other psychiatric disabilities must not be excluded from the trials that generate the claims and then targeted by the marketing that follows them - nor admitted, unscreened, to risks never measured in them.

    No commercial rollout in New York City. Allyspan opposes for-profit psychedelic clinics, patent-priced products and facilitator markets in this city while efficacy is unproven and the dangers in Sections 6 and 8 stand.

    Decriminalise people, do not commercialise supply. No one should be arrested, lose housing, benefits, work or children, or gain a record for possession. Ending punishment is not a licence to sell.

    Community need comes first. Public and charitable money should fund housing support, peer support, resource navigation, language access and proven tobacco, alcohol and mental-health care before any unproven intervention.

    If research continues, it answers to the community. Independent trials, no sponsor control of safety reporting, explicit and continuing consent, protection against abuse in altered states, long-term follow-up, publication of harms, and oversight that includes people with psychiatric disabilities themselves.

    Allyspan's test for any future proposal is simple: would this help a low-income New Yorker with schizophrenia,

    bipolar disorder or severe depression - safely, affordably and without a record or a bill they cannot pay? On the current evidence, commercial psychedelic therapy fails that test on every term.

    10   A community-centred model for New York City

    No community model of psychedelic access operates legally in New York City today, and no trial has tested such a model as a package. What follows is therefore a design argument built from the evidence in Sections 1-8, not a

    description of an existing service. It is consistent with Allyspan's position in Section 9, but it is presented here as a

    policy design for the city, not as an Allyspan service.

    First, decriminalise possession - and mean it. The lowest-priority resolution drafted by Decriminalize Nature

    NYC is the minimum legal step. To avoid repeating the marijuana pattern, it must come with a bar on using

    possession as a pretext for stops and searches, expungement of prior possession convictions, and protection against possession triggering child-welfare, housing or employment sanctions. A change in the law that leaves the collateral

    consequences intact changes little for the people most affected.

    Second, separate decriminalisation from commercialisation. Oregon shows that a licensed market without price

    controls becomes a $1,900-a-session boutique. Colorado shows that decriminalisation and a separate regulated track can coexist. For New York, the defensible sequence is decriminalisation now, and any future licensed delivery built

    as nonprofit or public at the point of care - with price caps and a public option through NYC Health + Hospitals or

    NYSPI - rather than a for-profit clinic market anchored to a $50,000-a-year drug price.

    Third, fund the support, not just the drug. Every positive trial bundled the drug with preparation, supervised

    dosing and integration - typically more than ten hours of skilled human time. The fungus is cheap; the care is

    expensive. A community model must fund peer supporters, integration circles and culturally competent therapists,

    on a sliding scale, with training scholarships instead of $7,000-$12,000 facilitator fees, and with paid roles for community health workers from the neighbourhoods most harmed by enforcement. Two existing models show the

    labour works: the Fireside Project's free, confidential psychedelic support line has handled more than 40,000

    conversations, and the Zendo Project's trained volunteers have sat with more than 7,000 people through difficult experiences at events. Both figures come from the organisations and secondary coverage and are given as reported,

    not independently audited.

    Fourth, screen hard - and publish the results. The trial exclusions should become community standards, applied by trained screeners with no financial interest in saying yes: psychosis and bipolar history, active suicidality,

    lithium, uncontrolled hypertension and interacting medications. Oregon's mystery-shopper study found licensed

    centres were in fact clinically cautious - none dosed without psychiatric assessment and none advised stopping

    antidepressants. A community model should be at least that careful, and should publish who it screens out, what adverse events occur, who uses the service, and what it costs, on a public dashboard, so that equity is audited rather

    than asserted.

    Fifth, build in Indigenous reciprocity, not acknowledgement. Paid Indigenous advisory roles with decision- making power, benefit-sharing, protection of ceremonial knowledge, and no patenting of traditional preparations. A

    community board with real authority - including Indigenous, Black, Latino, LGBTQ and peer voices - is a

    governance requirement, not a consultation exercise.

    Harm reduction has limits that should be stated in the same breath as its benefits. It cannot make psilocybin safe for a person with a history of psychosis, cannot verify the potency of an underground product, and cannot substitute for

    psychiatric care in severe depression or PTSD. A model that pretends otherwise will harm the people it claims to

    serve.

    11   Conclusion

    New York City is being asked to decide about psychedelics before the science has decided about psychedelics. The honest summary of the evidence is narrow. At Columbia, one well-run site contribution to a psilocybin depression

    trial and one well-designed ketamine pilot in addiction. Nationally, effects that point consistently in one direction

    and shrink under scrutiny, one regulatory refusal, and no approved psilocybin or MDMA product. In Albany and at City Hall, no law changed, no resolution passed, and four bills stalled. In the city's clinics and morgues, meanwhile,

    the substances that are already legal kill on a scale that dwarfs anything in this article - more than 480,000 tobacco

    deaths and about 178,000 alcohol deaths a year nationally - and concentrate their harm in people with mental illness, who smoke a third of the nation's cigarettes and die decades early.

    That contrast justifies reform of the legal hierarchy. It does not justify a market. The dangers set out in Sections 6

    and 8 fall on people with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, suicidality, eating disorders, dissociative disorders and

    complex prescriptions - people a commercial clinic can simply exclude, and a community service cannot. A model worthy of this city would decriminalise possession without commercialising supply, fund the human support that

    every successful trial actually consisted of, screen as rigorously as the trials do, publish its harms as openly as its

    hopes, share benefit with the Indigenous communities whose knowledge began this field, and refuse to let a $50,000 price tag decide whose depression counts. Until the evidence strengthens, that restraint is not a failure of

    ambition. It is the finding. Allyspan's position (Section 9) goes one step further, and is stated there as opinion rather

    than evidence: a community nonprofit founded on support, not on capitalism, should not lend its name to a market built on unproven treatment for the people it exists to stand beside.

    Limitations of this article

    This article relies on published trials, government statistics and, where labelled, company and trade-press reporting;

    phase 3 psilocybin results and the projected COMP360 price were not available in peer-reviewed or regulatory

    form at the time of writing. There is no representative New York City prevalence survey for psilocybin and no

    psilocybin-specific city arrest data, so national and sentinel-population figures are used as labelled proxies. Several risk estimates in Section 6 rest on case reports and self-selected surveys because controlled data in high-risk

    populations do not exist and, for ethical reasons, largely cannot be generated. Where a claim could not be verified -

    a Senate passage, a Council resolution, a nonprofit's FDA submission date - the article says so rather than repeating it.
